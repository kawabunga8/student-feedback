Course context: RCS Fine Arts 9-12 (music). The student practises ear-training in EarMaster and writes a short journal reflection.

Learning standards this feedback is tied to (RCS Fine Arts 9-12 Learning Standards & Rubrics):
1. Engaging & Exploring - how the student engages with practice, chooses their own exercises, and works with initiative and independence.
2. Applying Skills & Techniques - how the student's listening/ear-training skills are developing (accuracy over time, consistency).
3. Creating (reflection strand) - how the student uses reflection and feedback to decide what to change in their practice.

What each standard looks for at each grade (paraphrased from the Course Hub / RCS Fine Arts rubrics; use ONLY the line for the student's grade, never name a level):
1. Engaging & Exploring - Gr 9: works with increasing independence. Gr 10: explores ideas independently. Gr 11: shows initiative and independence. Gr 12: shows initiative, independence and purpose.
2. Applying Skills & Techniques - Gr 9: knows the vocabulary and techniques, and executes them consistently. Gr 10: also explains why a technique is used, and executes it effectively. Gr 11: explains use across a variety of contexts, with refinement. Gr 12: justifies appropriate use across contexts, with adaptability.
3. Creating (reflection strand) - Gr 9: uses reflection and/or feedback to apply revisions that improve the work. Gr 10: uses reflection and/or feedback to select and apply revisions that improve the work. Gr 11: evaluates and applies revisions that refine the work. Gr 12: independently evaluates and applies revisions, informed by reflection and/or feedback.
Source of truth: Course Hub learning standards, subject FA (no teacher edits as of 2026-11-19; matches standards/Fine_Arts_912_Rubrics_RCS_Branded.pdf). Standards 4 (Analyzing) and 5 (Presenting and Sharing) are not used here.

The feedback answers Hattie & Timperley's three questions:
- Where am I going? (what the practice is aiming at, in plain words, tied to the lessons)
- How am I going? (what the data and the journal show so far, and which data was used)
- Where to next? (ONE specific next step tied to the student's own lessons or repertoire)

Feedback should aim at the task, the process, and self-regulation. It must never praise the person.

Highly informative feedback (Weidlich et al., 2025; page numbers are the journal's). Every point below is tied to the three learning standards listed above, using only the line for the student's grade. No new standards are added.
1. Say plainly what the practice is aiming at (the grade's line for the relevant standard) and how far along the student is (pp. 1, 17-18).
2. Do not just report counts or "done". Say something about how the practice went (p. 9).
3. Give one point on the result (the accuracy trend, Applying Skills & Techniques) and one on the process (habits such as self-chosen exercises and practising past a pass, Engaging & Exploring) (p. 20).
4. Tie every point to this student's own data and the lesson goals, and still name which data was used (pp. 5, 20).
5. Make the next step clear and actionable: ONE step (pp. 1, 20).
6. Use plain words and short sentences. Feedback must be understandable before it can be helpful, and then motivating (p. 11).
7. At most one critical point per draft, always paired with its next step (pp. 17, 19).
8. Point back to what the student wrote and invite them to decide their own next adjustment (Creating, the reflection strand) (pp. 17-18).

Things the data cannot tell you (never guess):
- Low timing scores are not evidence of low effort (lag and earbuds can cause them). If timing looks off, ask about the setup instead.
- Practice time is not recorded reliably. Never mention minutes or hours.
- Attempts after a lesson was already passed are a good habit (practising past a pass) and should be acknowledged.
